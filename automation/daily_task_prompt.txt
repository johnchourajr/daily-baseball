===== TERMS (41) =====
0. Can of corn — An easily caught, high lazy fly ball that lets a fielder camp under it. (first used 1926)
1. Baltimore chop — A ball chopped down near the plate that bounces so high the batter can beat it out for a hit.
2. Texas Leaguer — A cheap hit that loops just over the infielders but drops in front of the outfielders. (first used 1892)
3. Southpaw — A left-handed pitcher or thrower. (first used 1869)
4. Rhubarb — A noisy on-field argument or ruckus with the umpires.
5. Bullpen — The area where relief pitchers warm up during a game — and, by extension, a team's whole relief staff.
6. Ace — A team's best pitcher, usually a starter and the rock of the rotation. (Earliest sense: a run or score in 1840s baseball.) (first used 1902)
7. Dinger — A home run. (first used 1974)
8. Tater — A home run.
9. Dying quail — A pop fly that drops suddenly, like a bird shot on the wing, often falling between fielders for a hit. (first used 1947)
10. Frozen rope — A hard-hit line drive on a rigid, low, straight path that seems to defy gravity. (first used 1938)
11. Gopher ball — A pitch destined to be hit for a home run — one that will 'go for' extra bases. (first used 1930)
12. Hot corner — Third base. (first used 1889)
13. Moon shot — A home run, especially one hit a very long distance. (first used 1959)
14. Pepper — A fast, close-range pregame drill where a batter taps quick grounders to a cluster of fielders who fire the ball right back. (first used 1914)
15. Seeing-eye single — A softly hit single that sneaks through the infield as if the ball had eyes.
16. Shoestring catch — A running catch made at the fielder's shoe tops, just before the ball hits the ground. (first used 1904)
17. Twin killing — A double play. (first used 1920)
18. Wheelhouse — The part of the strike zone where a batter swings with the most power; hang a pitch there and invite a home run. (first used 1959)
19. Cup of coffee — A brief trial in the major leagues by a minor-league player — figuratively, just long enough to drink a cup of coffee. (first used 1908)
20. Uncle Charlie — A curveball, especially a big sweeping one. (first used 1933)
21. Cheese — A fastball. 'High cheese' is one up and in. (first used 1975)
22. Chin music — A brushback pitch thrown so high and inside the batter can supposedly hear it buzz past his jaw. (first used 1867)
23. Circus catch — A spectacular catch — a jump, dive, flip, or roll — suggesting a circus acrobat. (first used 1878)
24. Cellar — Last place — the position held by the team with the worst record. (first used 1907)
25. Closer — The relief pitcher used to get the final outs and protect a late lead. (first used 1977)
26. Crooked number — Any inning total greater than one — so called for the curves in the numerals 2 through 9. (first used 1978)
27. Ducks on the pond — Baserunners waiting to be driven home. (first used 1921)
28. Golden sombrero — The dubious mark for striking out four times in a single game. (first used 1979)
29. Mendoza Line — The .200 batting-average threshold separating adequate hitters from the rest. (first used 1982)
30. Meatball — An easy-to-hit pitch served right down the middle. (first used 1912)
31. Immaculate inning — A half-inning in which a pitcher strikes out all three batters on exactly nine pitches. (first used 2008)
32. Warning track — The dirt or cinder strip inside the outfield wall that warns a fielder, by feel underfoot, that the wall is near.
33. Worm burner — A ball hit so hard and low it skids across the ground without bouncing.
34. Pickle — A rundown — a baserunner trapped between two bases with fielders closing in. (Also, as a verb, to hit a ball very hard.) (first used 1908)
35. Bandbox — A ballpark whose small dimensions make home runs easy — John Updike's 'lyric little green bandbox' was Fenway. (first used 1894)
36. Submariner — A pitcher who throws with an underhand, submarine-style delivery.
37. Payoff pitch — The pitch thrown on a full count (3-2) — barring a foul, it decides the at-bat.
38. Spitball — A late-breaking pitch thrown off a ball loaded with saliva, banned in 1920 for hygiene and to boost offense. (first used 1902)
39. Knuckleball — A slow pitch thrown with almost no spin so it flutters, darts, and dips unpredictably — sometimes moving 11 inches. (first used 1907)
40. Screwball — A pitch that breaks the opposite way from a curveball, thrown with an inward twist of the wrist. (Also: a zany player.) (first used 1921)

===== RULES (25) — render as "RULE CHANGE — <year>" =====
0. 1857 :: A game used to end after the inning in which a team reached 21 runs. This year it became a nine-inning contest won by the higher score.
1. 1858 :: Called strikes are introduced — before this, a batter could simply wait for a pitch he liked.
2. 1858 :: A batter is out on a batted ball, fair or foul, if it is caught on the fly OR after one bounce.
3. 1863 :: Called balls and bases on balls are introduced.
4. 1864 :: The one-bounce out on fair balls is abolished; a fair ball must now be caught on the fly.
5. 1868 :: Bat length is limited to 42 inches — still the limit today.
6. 1870 :: The batter-runner is allowed to overrun first base.
7. 1871 :: The batter is officially allowed to call for a high or low pitch (a practice since 1864).
8. 1873 :: The batter's box is introduced.
9. 1877 :: Canvas bases 15 inches square are introduced, and home plate is placed in the angle formed by the first- and third-base lines.
10. 1878 :: It took NINE called balls to draw a walk.
11. 1880 :: A walk drops to seven called balls, and the batter no longer gets a fourth strike.
12. 1883 :: Six called balls become a base on balls.
13. 1885 :: One side of the bat is allowed to be flat — a rule rescinded in 1893.
14. 1887 :: Four called strikes are adopted — for this season only. Walks are also recorded as hits, also for this season only.
15. 1887 :: The batter is awarded first base when hit by a pitch.
16. 1887 :: The strike zone is established between the shoulders and the knees.
17. 1889 :: Four balls finally become a base on balls — the modern standard.
18. 1891 :: Large padded mitts are allowed for catchers.
19. 1893 :: The pitching box is eliminated for a rubber slab, moved from 50 feet to 60 feet 6 inches — the distance still used today.
20. 1894 :: Foul bunts are classified as strikes.
21. 1895 :: The infield-fly rule is adopted.
22. 1900 :: Home plate changes from a square to the five-sided figure used today.
23. 1901 :: Foul balls start counting as strikes until the batter has two strikes.
24. 1903 :: The height of the mound is limited to 15 inches above the baselines.
